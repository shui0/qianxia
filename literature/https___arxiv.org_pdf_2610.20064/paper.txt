Abstract — We present FoveaVLA, a Vision-Language-Action (VLA)   model   that   replaces   the   wrist   camera   with   learned foveation actions. Wrist cameras provide close-up detail but restrict the view to the end-effector’s surroundings. External cameras see the whole scene, but the policy receives them at low resolution, so the fine detail needed for manipulation is lost. FoveaVLA recovers this detail by predicting, alongside each action chunk, the interaction region to observe at the next policy query. The policy then receives crops of these regions, taken from the original-resolution images, together with the full views. We build FoveaVLA on   π 0 . 5   in two configurations, Single and Dual, using one or two external stereo cameras with full and foveal RGB and depth inputs, and post-train both on DROID with RoboInter interaction annotations. Across four real-world task suites, FoveaVLA-Single achieves   70 . 8 % success with one external RGB-D view, and FoveaVLA-Dual achieves  77 . 1 %, versus   52 . 1 % for the wrist-equipped   π 0 . 5 . Both match the baseline on plain pick-and-place and improve success with distractors, cloth manipulation, and far-away objects.  I. I NTRODUCTION  Recent progress in robot manipulation has been driven by large models trained on diverse robot datasets [1]–[3]. Most of these models take a wrist camera as input [4]–[6], which provides close-up observations that learned policies often depend on [7]–[9]. This close-up view comes at the cost of scene coverage: the camera moves with the end- effector, so distant targets and surrounding context can lie outside its view, more so when manipulation spans a larger  † Equal advising. The wrist camera also adds hardware and creates an observation mismatch with human demonstrations recorded without a wrist view [10], [11]. Can a policy get the close-up detail it needs from external observations alone, so that the robot no longer needs a wrist camera? External cameras record high-resolution images, but re- sizing a whole scene to the policy’s low input resolution reduces the detail available for the gripper and object, and uniformly increasing the input resolution spends computation on background regions. External views also lack a consistent geometric frame: a wrist camera maintains a fixed pose relative to the end-effector, whereas an external view depends on the camera’s placement relative to the robot. Prior work reports   lower   success   for   policies   learned   without   wrist observations [7], [12]. We observe a similar gap on our four real-world task suites:   π 0 . 5   post-trained on two external RGB views alone achieves   25 . 0 % success, compared with   52 . 1 % with the wrist view. Existing approaches obtain close-up observations through additional   sensing   or   constructed   viewpoints.   Generative methods   synthesize   wrist   images   from   external   observa- tions [13], [14]. Others render virtual views from 3D scene representations [15], [16], or move a camera on a dedicated arm or neck [17], [18]. Human-gaze-guided foveation is closer to our goal of selecting detail within an existing image [19], [20], but relies on gaze recorded during data col- lection. We instead learn where to crop from existing robot demonstrations, supervised by their interaction annotations. arXiv:2610.20064v2 [cs.RO] 21 Oct 2026

Human vision allocates detail selectively. Peripheral vision provides scene context while central vision resolves the detail needed to grasp an object [21], and gaze reaches the next contact point before the hand does [22]. We take the same approach in FoveaVLA, which replaces the wrist camera with a learned foveation action that selects where to look next. Cropping the predicted interaction region from the original-resolution image and resizing it to the same  224   ×   224   encoder input devotes many more pixels to the gripper and object than the resized full view does. Feeding this crop alongside the full view gives the policy both the close-up detail it needs for manipulation and the layout of the whole scene. We   build   FoveaVLA   on   π 0 . 5   [23].   From   the   current observation, it generates a robot action chunk and predicts one bounding box per external view for the interaction region at the next policy query. The predicted boxes crop the newly captured images, and the crops are added to the next policy input alongside the full views. We derive supervision from RoboInter annotations [24] on DROID demonstrations [5], constructing a bounding box around the components involved in each annotated skill, such as the gripper, object, and target region. Annotations at the next query provide targets for   future-box   prediction.   We   instantiate   the   method   as FoveaVLA-Single and FoveaVLA-Dual, using one and two external stereo cameras, respectively, with full and foveal RGB images and aligned depth estimated from each stereo pair. Both operate without a wrist camera; Dual adds a second external viewpoint to the same foveation mechanism. We post-train both configurations on DROID and evaluate them on the four real-world task suites. FoveaVLA-Single achieves   70 . 8 % success using one external RGB-D view, exceeding the wrist-equipped   π 0 . 5   at   52 . 1 %, and FoveaVLA- Dual achieves   77 . 1 %. The largest gains are on pick-and- place with distractors, where Single raises success from  25 . 0 % to   58 . 3 %, and on moving far objects, from   41 . 7 % to   75 . 0 %. On MolmoSpaces [25], FoveaVLA-Dual achieves  22 . 4 % average zero-shot success versus   17 . 8 % for   π 0 . 5 - DROID [26], outperforming it on seven of nine tasks. II. R ELATED   W ORK  Wrist cameras in robot manipulation.   Eye-in-hand cam- eras date back to visual servoing [27] and were adopted in learning-based manipulation after empirical studies showed their benefit [12]. Hsu et al. argued that the partial ob- servability of the hand-centric view is an inductive bias that aids generalization [7]. Wrist cameras are now standard in bimanual hardware [4], handheld collection devices [6], datasets and benchmarks [5], [25], [28], and generalist poli- cies [23], [29], [30]. Wrist-only observations can lack the scene context needed for manipulation, motivating a wide- angle fisheye lens in UMI [6] and an additional external view in MV-UMI [31]. Policies also come to depend on this view: LIBERO-Plus finds that multi-view VLAs retain much of their performance with the external view masked [8], and other studies find policies including   π 0 . 5   more sensitive to masking or perturbing the wrist view [9], [32], [33]. FoveaVLA keeps the close-up that makes the wrist view valuable, without mounting a camera on the hand.  Constructed viewpoints and active vision.   WristWorld generates wrist-view videos from anchor views [13], while inference-time view synthesis supplies in-hand images to a trained policy [14]. WARPED renders wrist-aligned training observations from egocentric human videos but retains a physical wrist camera during execution [34]. Others re-render the scene from virtual viewpoints using 3D scene representa- tions, zooming coarse to fine on point clouds [16] or selecting virtual views and applying 3D zoom within a VLA [15]. Physical active-vision systems instead control a camera using demonstrations [17], [18], [35] or task reward [36]. EyeVLA predicts pan, tilt, and zoom for a physical PTZ camera [37]. Such systems require camera actuation and supervision or rewards for camera control. FoveaVLA uses fixed cameras and selects regions within the images they already capture.  Learned visual attention and foveation.   Human vision resolves fine detail only in a small central region, and the eyes move this region to the grasp point before the hand arrives   [21],   [22],   [38].   Attention   models   learn   glimpse actions [39], and foveation also aids recognition [40] and gaze-conditioned generation [41]. VLMs retrieve local ev- idence through crops [42]–[45] to answer questions about a given image, whereas we use predicted regions to select the next robot observation. In manipulation, foveation has been supervised by human gaze, as a foveal crop fed to the policy [19] or gaze co-predicted with actions [20], a signal recorded with eye trackers during collection and not provided by DROID. AtVLA corrects visual attention with register tokens and, when uncertain, crops a region chosen by attention rollout [46]. Another approach trains a policy that chooses which regions a sensor captures for frozen downstream models, including a manipulation policy [47]. FoveaVLA instead decodes a future interaction box alongside each action chunk. Prior VLAs also use localization super- vision [23], spatial traces [48], [49], or visual and textual affordances [50], but only as auxiliary targets that help action prediction. In FoveaVLA, the predicted box determines the crop the policy receives next, and its supervision comes from existing large-scale interaction annotations [24]. III. P RELIMINARIES  Vision-Language-Action   models.   A   VLA   is   a   robot policy   π θ   ( A t   |   o t , ℓ )   that conditions on the current obser- vation   o t , comprising camera images and the robot state  q t , and a language instruction   ℓ . It outputs an action chunk  A t   = ( a t +1 , . . . , a t + H   )   spanning a prediction horizon of   H  control steps. In receding-horizon execution, the controller can execute only the first   w   ≤   H   actions before replanning. A common recipe starts from a pretrained vision-language model, adds an action head, and trains the resulting policy by imitation on robot demonstrations [2], [3]. The visual encoder converts camera images into patch tokens, which the language-model backbone processes together with the instruction and robot state to form the representation used for action generation.

Backbone and action generation.   We build on   π 0 . 5   [23]. Its PaliGemma backbone [51] combines a SigLIP visual encoder with a Gemma language model, while a separate action expert generates continuous action chunks through flow matching, conditioned on the observation representa- tion. PaliGemma’s vocabulary also includes location tokens for image coordinates in detection and grounding. The   π 0 . 5  base checkpoint does not emit these tokens at inference; we reuse them to represent foveation actions in Sec. IV-B. IV. M ETHOD  FoveaVLA learns where to look alongside how to act (Fig. 2). From the full and foveal RGB-D observations, the robot state, and a task instruction, it predicts an action chunk and the interaction region for the next observation. The predicted regions determine which crops of the next external images the policy receives. We post-train it from the   π 0 . 5   base checkpoint on DROID with RoboInter anno- tations. FoveaVLA-Single uses one external stereo camera, and FoveaVLA-Dual uses two.  A. Full and Foveal Observations  Let   N   ∈ { 1 ,   2 }   be the number of external views for Single and Dual, respectively. Let   I v t   be the source RGB image, at the camera’s original resolution, from external view   v   ∈  V   =   { 1 , . . . , N   }   at time   t , and let   b v t   specify the region selected for detailed observation. The full view   G v t   =   R ( I v t   )  is the source image resized and padded to the encoder’s low input resolution ( 224   ×   224   in our case). The foveal crop  F   v t   =   C ( I v t   , b v t   )   is a square crop around   b v t   , extracted at the source resolution and resized to the same low resolution. The full view preserves the scene layout, while the foveal crop spends the same number of pixels on the interaction region. Each external camera is a stereo camera. We use Fast- FoundationStereo [52] to estimate disparity from its rectified image pair and convert it to metric depth using the focal length and stereo baseline. The depth map   D v t   is aligned with   I v t   and provides an explicit distance cue. We apply the same resizing and cropping to depth as to RGB, giving   D v G,t  and   D v F,t . Depth is mapped through a fixed logarithmic scale and colorized for the visual encoder; patches with insuffi- cient valid depth are masked. The resulting observation is  o t   =    q t ,    G v t   , F   v t   , D v G,t , D v F,t  N v =1    ,   where   q t   contains the robot’s joint positions and gripper state. RGB and colorized depth use the same visual encoder and enter the transformer as separate sequences of visual tokens. The task instruction   ℓ  and discretized robot state complete the shared input prefix.  B. Foveation Actions  We extend the VLA policy defined in Sec. III to predict one bounding box per external view alongside the robot action chunk. Each box describes the interaction region at  t   +   w , when the policy is queried next after executing the first   w   actions. We predict   H   = 16   actions and execute  w   = 8   per query (Appendix B). We target   t   +   w   because the gripper and objects move during the executed actions, and apply the predicted box to the image captured at that time. Denoting   B t + w   =   { b v t + w } v ∈ V   for the foveation actions, the joint policy factorizes as  π θ   ( A t , B t + w   |   o t , ℓ ) =   π θ   ( A t   |   o t , ℓ )   p θ   ( B t + w   |   o t , ℓ ) .   (1) The two outputs share the observation representation, but are predicted by two independent heads: the action expert generates   A t , and the language-model head decodes   B t + w . We initialize FoveaVLA from the   π 0 . 5   base checkpoint, retaining its backbone and flow-matching action expert. To predict   B t + w , we reuse the language-model output head to autoregressively decode the existing location tokens intro- duced in Sec. III. Each box is represented by four coordinates on the resized and padded full view, quantized into   1 , 024   bins and seri- alized in   ( y min , x min , y max , x max )   order. The boxes form a sequence of   4 N   location tokens: four for Single and eight for Dual. The output vocabulary is restricted to location tokens. The action expert attends to the shared input prefix, while each box token also attends to the preceding box tokens. The two output branches are masked from each other. Learning from Interaction Annotations  We derive foveation supervision from the RoboInter an- notations associated with DROID demonstrations [5], [24]. For each annotated frame and external view, we construct one interaction box as the smallest axis-aligned rectangle enclos- ing the components required by the annotated primitive skill. Figure 3 illustrates transfer and fold; both use the gripper, manipulated object, and annotated target region. Appendix A specifies the complete role mapping and preprocessing pro- cedure. The interaction box defines the supervision target; the foveal crop is a square region enclosing this box, extracted as described in Sec. IV-A. A training example pairs the observation and demonstrated action chunk at   t   with the interaction boxes at   t   +   w . To improve robustness to imperfect predictions, we augment ground-truth crops at   t   by perturbing their centers and scales while retaining the annotated region. Some crops are replaced by full views to match the initial query at inference. RGB and depth share the same crop geometry. Training examples require valid current and future annotations in the selected view for Single and in both views for Dual. During post-training, we jointly optimize action genera- tion and foveation prediction. For the action objective, we interpolate between a demonstrated chunk   A t   and Gaussian noise   ϵ   ∼ N   (0 , I )   as   A τ t   = (1 − τ   ) A t + τ ϵ , where   τ   ∈   [0 ,   1]   is the flow time. Subsequent foveal inputs use the preceding box predictions to crop the current RGB images and aligned depth maps, as described in Sec. IV-B. Each policy query encodes the observation prefix once for both outputs. The action expert generates the chunk by integrating the learned flow from noise to actions, and the chunk is returned immediately; the bounding box location tokens are then decoded greedily from the cached prefix while the robot executes the first   w   actions, so the boxes are ready when the next frames arrive at   t   +   w . A PPENDIX   A D ATASET   C ONSTRUCTION  A. Matching demonstrations and annotations  We construct training examples by joining DROID demon- strations with RoboInter’s frame-level interaction annota- tions [5], [24]. DROID’s RLDS records provide the robot state, action sequence, and task instruction; the correspond- ing raw recordings provide external-camera images at   1280 ×  720   resolution and stereo recordings for depth estimation. We match the two by episode, camera view, and frame index, so each crop, box target, and robot action come from the same timestep. We use the rectified left imager of each external stereo camera as the RGB reference. The wrist stream is not used to construct FoveaVLA’s observations.  B. Interaction-box construction  An interaction box encloses the visual components re- quired by the annotated primitive skill. We use three an- notation roles: the gripper, the manipulated object or tool, and the target region, which RoboInter labels as a placement proposal. Frames with a missing or invalid required component, or an unsupported primitive, are excluded. Primitive labels are used to construct supervision and are not provided to FoveaVLA at inference.  C. Temporal pairing and sample validity  A sample at   t   uses   b v t   to construct its foveal observation and   b v t +8   as the prediction target ( w   = 8 ). Both annotations must   be   valid,   and   the   future   frame   must   exist   in   the same demonstration. The future box is constructed using the primitive annotated at the future frame, which allows the target to change across a skill transition. We intersect these eligible samples with the DROID non-idle timestep ranges used by the OpenPI training pipeline [26] and with the availability of aligned depth. Single uses the physical- left external view; Dual additionally requires valid inputs and targets for the physical-right view, paired with the left view at the same timestep.  D. Foveal crops and augmentation  The prediction target is the tight interaction box; the visual encoder receives a larger square crop around it. Let   m   be the larger of the box’s pixel width and height. The nominal crop is centered on the box and has side length   1 . 5 m . We adjust its position and size at image boundaries while retaining the interaction box. When a square containing the box cannot fit inside the image, the out-of-image area is padded with zeros. The crop is extracted from the   1280   ×   720   source image and then resized to   224   ×   224 . Full views preserve aspect ratio and are padded to the same encoder resolution. The prepared training crops use a mixture of   45%   nominal crops,   45%   perturbed crops, and   10%   full views. For a perturbed crop, each center coordinate is shifted uniformly by up to   0 . 25 m , and the nominal side length is multiplied by a log-uniform factor in   [1 ,   4 / 3] . The resulting square is constrained to retain the annotated interaction region. The full views match the initial inference query, before any predicted box is available. The prediction target remains the unperturbed future box.  E. Aligned metric depth  For each external camera, we recover its rectified stereo pair and camera calibration from the raw DROID recording. Fast-FoundationStereo [52] estimates disparity from each camera’s own stereo pair at   640 × 360   resolution, using eight refinement iterations and a maximum disparity of   192 . After accounting for the resize scale, metric optical-axis depth is computed as   z   =   f x B/d , where   f x   is the horizontal focal length,   B   is the stereo baseline in meters, and   d   is disparity in pixels at the corresponding resolution. We   mark   non-finite   or   non-positive   disparity,   corre- spondences   outside   the   right   image,   and   depths   outside  [0 . 2 ,   3 . 0]   m as invalid. The depth map is aligned to the ref- erence RGB image, and the same padding and stored foveal crop coordinates are applied to depth and its validity mask. Real-robot deployment uses the same disparity conversion and crop geometry. A PPENDIX   B I MPLEMENTATION AND   T RAINING   D ETAILS  A. The learning rate warms up to   5 × 10 − 5   over the first   1 , 000  updates and remains constant thereafter. We use the DROID normalization statistics supplied with the base checkpoint.

Following the base implementation, flow time is sampled as   τ   = 0 . 999 u   + 0 . 001   with   u   ∼  Beta(1 . 5 ,   1) , and inference integrates the flow from noise to actions with ten steps. Task instances and execution  The real-world benchmark contains four task suites with  12   instances each. An instance specifies the objects, their initial placement, and a language instruction; the same in- stances are used across methods. Our tasks adapt the tabletop tasks of Cloak [56], with changes to the object set and towel setups and a Pick-and-Place with Distractors suite in place of Cloak’s Remove task. Tables VII–X list all   48   instances. Figure 6 shows representative initial and completed states from all four suites. Figure 7 illustrates the full views and foveal crops during a successful distractor trial. We use the DROID robot setup described in Sec. V-A. Each trial runs at a target   15   Hz for at most   900   control steps and can be stopped earlier to avoid robot damage. The object lists and positions are used only to set up the scene and are not given to the policy. Positions are named tabletop slots: FL, FR, BL, and BR denote front-left, front-right, back- left, and back-right, respectively, and FM and BM denote the middle position in the front and back rows.  B. Task definitions and success criteria a) Pick-and-Place:   The policy must move the specified source object into the specified container. The instances vary the source among markers, cubes, and towels and the container among bowls, a box, and a large pot. Success requires grasping the specified source and releasing it inside the correct container.  b) Move:   The policy must move the source object next to a reference object. Success requires grasping the source and releasing it within one source-object width of the reference without moving the reference. Instances use cubes, cups, and towels as sources and cups, bowls, and a large pot as references.  c) Fold/Unfold:   The   12   trials comprise four groups of three: folding one towel, folding a target towel with a distractor present, unfolding one towel, and unfolding a target towel with a distractor present. Each group targets blue, grey, and pink towels once each. For folding, every towel in the scene starts flat; for unfolding, every towel starts with one half-fold. Thus, in the two-towel scenes, initial fold state does not reveal the target. The instruction specifies the target color. Folding requires one half-fold with opposite edges aligned; unfolding requires a flat towel with no folded-over section. Success requires completing the requested manipulation on the specified towel and releasing it.  d) Pick-and-Place with Distractors:   The four scene compositions are two markers with one bowl, two markers with two bowls, three markers with two bowls, and three markers with three bowls, with three trials per composition. Markers occupy the back row and empty bowls the front row. A two-object row uses its left and right positions; a three- object row additionally uses the middle position, which is occupied by a distractor. A single bowl is placed at front- middle. The instruction names both the marker and bowl colors, and each group uses all three marker colors and all three bowl colors once as targets. Success requires placing the instructed marker inside the instructed bowl.  C. Outcome annotation  An operator assigns binary success after each attempt according to the task-specific criteria above. In scenes with distractors, disturbing a distractor is allowed as long as the instructed manipulation is completed. Per-suite success is averaged over its   12   trials, and Overall pools all   48   trials, with SEM reported as in the main text.  D. Complete trial specifications  The tables list the exact instructions given to the policy, which sometimes use alternative words such as “pen” for a marker and “cloth” for a towel. Within the Fold/Unfold and Distractors suites, only the objects listed for an instance are placed on the table. The Fold/Unfold initial-state rules above apply to both the target and any distractor towel.
